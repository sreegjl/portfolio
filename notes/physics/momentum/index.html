impulse & momentum

linear momentum, impulse, conservation of momentum, and center of mass
study mode

Linear momentum

The linear momentum of an object is the product of its mass and velocity, a measure of how hard it is to stop:

\[\class{var-p}{\vec{p}} = m\vec{v}\]

Variables

\(\vec{p}\) momentum vector (kg·m/s)
\(m\) mass (kg)
\(\vec{v}\) velocity vector (m/s)
Units kg·m/s \(=\) N·s

Key properties

Type vector, points along \(\vec{v}\)
Components \(p_x = mv_x\), \(p_y = mv_y\), tracked separately
Frame dependence depends on the observer's reference frame
vs. kinetic energy \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\), scalar, momentum keeps direction

Newton's second law, as he originally wrote it: net force equals the rate of change of momentum:

\[\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \dfrac{\Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}}}{\Delta t}\]

For constant mass this reduces to the familiar \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a}\); the momentum form also handles changing mass, like a rocket burning fuel.

Impulse

Impulse is the effect of a force accumulated over time. For a constant (or average) force:

\[\class{var-j}{\vec{J}} = \vec{F}\,\Delta t\]

Rearranging Newton's second law gives the impulse-momentum theorem: impulse from the net force equals the change in momentum:

\[\class{var-j}{\vec{J}_{\text{net}}} = \vec{F}_{\text{net}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}} = m\vec{v}_f - m\vec{v}_i\]

Variables

\(\vec{J}\) impulse vector (N·s)
\(\vec{F}\) force, use the average if it varies (N)
\(\Delta t\) duration of the interaction (s)
Units N·s \(=\) kg·m/s, same as momentum

Key properties

Type vector, points along the net force
F vs. t graph impulse \(=\) area under the curve
Same \(\Delta p\), longer \(\Delta t\) smaller force
Same \(\Delta p\), shorter \(\Delta t\) larger force

This trade-off is why airbags, crumple zones, and bending your knees on a landing work: \(\Delta p\) is fixed by the situation, so stretching out \(\Delta t\) shrinks the force.

Force vs. time graphs

Real forces are rarely constant. On a force vs. time graph, the impulse, and therefore the change in momentum, is the area between the force curve and the t-axis:

\[\class{var-j}{J} = \text{area under the } F \text{ vs. } t \text{ curve} = \Delta \class{var-p}{p}\]

Reading the graph

Constant force rectangle, \(J = F\Delta t\)
Linearly changing force trapezoid or triangle, \(J = \tfrac{1}{2}(F_1 + F_2)\Delta t\)
Area above the axis positive impulse momentum increases
Area below the axis negative impulse momentum decreases
Average force \(F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p / \Delta t\), the constant force with the same area

Try the sliders below: the shaded area is the impulse, which equals \(\Delta \class{var-p}{p}\), and dividing by the mass gives \(\Delta v\).

Area under a force-time graph

A real collision force is a sharp spike you usually cannot read exactly, but the area still equals \(\Delta \class{var-p}{p}\): measure the momenta before and after to get the impulse, and \(F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p / \Delta t\) recovers the average force.

Conservation of momentum

By Newton's third law, interacting objects exchange equal and opposite impulses: whatever momentum one gains, the other loses. So if the net external force on a system is zero, its total momentum cannot change:

\[\sum \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_i} = \sum \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_f}\]

For two objects this reads:

\[m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i} = m_1\vec{v}_{1f} + m_2\vec{v}_{2f}\]

Applying conservation

Condition \(\vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}} = 0\), internal forces never change total \(\vec{p}\)
Component form \(p_x\) and \(p_y\) are conserved independently
Brief, violent interactions collision forces dwarf gravity or friction, so \(\vec{p}\) is conserved during the impact
System choice include both colliding objects, a force is only "internal" if both ends are inside

When the net external force is nonzero, the environment transfers momentum to or from the system through the external impulse, the impulse-momentum theorem applied to the whole system:

\[\class{var-j}{\vec{J}_{\text{ext}}} = \vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_{\text{sys}}}\]

Closed vs. open systems

\(\vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}} = 0\) \(\Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_{\text{sys}}} = 0\) total momentum is constant
\(\vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}} \neq 0\) \(\Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_{\text{sys}}} = \class{var-j}{\vec{J}_{\text{ext}}}\) momentum transferred between system and environment
Redraw the boundary put the force's source inside the system and total \(\vec{p}\) is conserved again

The boundary you draw decides the case. A falling ball alone is open: gravity is external and Earth feeds it momentum. Include the Earth and gravity becomes internal: the ball gains exactly what the Earth loses. Momentum is never created or destroyed, only moved across the boundary.

Center of mass

The center of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system. For a set of point masses:

\[x_{\text{cm}} = \dfrac{\sum m_i x_i}{\sum m_i} = \dfrac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + \cdots}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots}\]

Its velocity connects directly to total momentum:

\[\vec{v}_{\text{cm}} = \dfrac{\sum m_i \vec{v}_i}{\sum m_i} = \dfrac{\class{var-p}{\vec{p}_{\text{tot}}}}{M}\]

Key properties

Total momentum \(\vec{p}_{\text{tot}} = M\vec{v}_{\text{cm}}\), the system moves like one particle of mass \(M\)
Newton's second law \(\vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}} = M\vec{a}_{\text{cm}}\), internal forces cannot accelerate the CM
No external force \(\vec{v}_{\text{cm}}\) is constant, even during collisions or explosions
Location sits closer to the heavier mass, can lie outside any object (a donut, a boomerang)

This is why firework fragments spread out around the path the intact shell would have followed: the explosion is internal, so the center of mass keeps tracing the original parabola. Try the sliders below: launch a shell and burst it at the apex.

Firework shell bursting at the apex

No matter how lopsided the split, the purple center of mass never leaves the original path: internal forces cannot move it.

This makes the center of mass a problem-solving tool: track it instead of the pieces. Suppose an astronaut and a cargo crate drift together through space at \(3\ \text{m/s}\), starting from the origin. At \(t = 4\ \text{s}\) the astronaut shoves the crate ahead, and at \(t = 6\ \text{s}\) the crate is at \(24\ \text{m}\). The astronaut is three times as massive as the crate. Where is the astronaut?

Worked example: astronaut and crate

The shove is internal no external force in space, so \(\vec{v}_{\text{cm}}\) stays at \(3\ \text{m/s}\) the whole time
Track the CM \(x_{\text{cm}} = (3\ \text{m/s})(6\ \text{s}) = 18\ \text{m}\) at \(t = 6\ \text{s}\)
Weighted average \(x_{\text{cm}} = \dfrac{3m \, x_{\text{astro}} + m \, x_{\text{crate}}}{4m} \implies 18 = \dfrac{3 x_{\text{astro}} + 24}{4}\)
Solve \(x_{\text{astro}} = 16\ \text{m}\), behind the CM, exactly the recoil the shove demands

Notice what we never needed: the force of the push, how long it lasted, or either skater's velocity afterward. The center of mass ignores all of it and keeps gliding.

Key takeaways

Summary

Momentum \(\class{var-p}{\vec{p}} = m\vec{v}\), vector, units kg·m/s
Newton's second law \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}} / \Delta t\), general form of \(F = ma\)
Impulse \(\class{var-j}{\vec{J}} = \vec{F}\Delta t\), area under the \(F\) vs. \(t\) curve
Impulse-momentum theorem \(\class{var-j}{\vec{J}_{\text{net}}} = \Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}}\), longer contact time means smaller force
Conservation total \(\vec{p}\) is constant when \(\vec{F}_{\text{ext,net}} = 0\), each component separately
Nonzero external force \(\class{var-j}{\vec{J}_{\text{ext}}} = \Delta \class{var-p}{\vec{p}_{\text{sys}}}\), momentum transferred to or from the environment
Center of mass \(\vec{p}_{\text{tot}} = M\vec{v}_{\text{cm}}\), internal forces never accelerate it
