torque & rotational dynamics

torque, rotational inertia, rotational dynamics, angular momentum, and angular impulse
study mode

Torque

A force changes how an object moves; a torque changes how it rotates. Torque measures how effectively a force twists an object about an axis, and it depends on how much force you apply, how far from the axis you apply it, and at what angle:

\[\class{var-tau}{\tau} = rF\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta}\]

Variables

\(\tau\) torque (N·m)
\(r\) distance from the axis to where the force acts (m)
\(F\) applied force (N)
\(\theta\) angle between \(\vec{r}\) and \(\vec{F}\)

Worked example: stuck bolt

Given \(80\ \text{N}\) on a \(0.25\ \text{m}\) wrench, perpendicular to the handle
Torque \(\tau = (0.25)(80)\sin 90^\circ = 20\ \text{N·m}\)
Same push at \(30^\circ\) \(\tau = (0.25)(80)\sin 30^\circ = 10\ \text{N·m}\) half as effective
Fix push perpendicular, or use a longer wrench to grow \(r\)

The quantity \(r\sin\theta\) is the lever arm: the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action. A force pointed straight at the axis (\(\theta = 0\)) has zero lever arm and produces no torque, which is why pushing a door at its hinges gets you nowhere. Like angular velocity, torque carries a sign: counterclockwise positive, clockwise negative.

Force diagrams for rotation

Two kids on a seesaw

A regular free-body diagram shrinks the object to a dot, but a dot cannot show where a force acts, and for torque, location is everything. Rotation problems use an extended free-body diagram: draw the object as a shape, mark the axis of rotation, and attach each force arrow at its actual point of application.

Building an extended free-body diagram

1. Sketch the shape a bar, disk, or beam, not a point; mark the pivot or axis
2. Place each force draw it at the point where it acts, with its true direction
Gravity acts at the center of mass; for a uniform beam, the midpoint
Pivot forces act at the axis, so \(r = 0\) and they contribute zero torque
3. Sum the torques \(\tau_\text{net} = \sum \tau\), counterclockwise positive

Picking the pivot as your axis is the classic trick: any unknown force acting there drops out of the torque equation. If \(\tau_\text{net} = 0\) the object is in rotational equilibrium, like a balanced seesaw where the child sitting closer to the pivot must weigh more for the twists to cancel.

Rotational inertia

Mass measures resistance to being accelerated; rotational inertia (moment of inertia) measures resistance to being spun up. It depends not just on how much mass an object has but on how far that mass sits from the axis:

\[\class{var-inertia}{I} = \sum m r^2\]

Common shapes (axis through center)

Point mass, hoop \(I = MR^2\) all mass far out
Solid disk or cylinder \(I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2\)
Solid sphere \(I = \tfrac{2}{5}MR^2\) mass hugs the axis
Thin rod, center / end \(I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2\) / \(I = \tfrac{1}{3}ML^2\)

Key properties

Grows with \(r^2\) doubling a mass's distance from the axis quadruples its contribution
Depends on the axis the same rod is four times harder to spin about its end than its center
Adds up total \(I\) is the sum over every piece of the system
Units kg·m²

A hoop and a disk of equal mass and radius are not equal rotationally, since the hoop keeps all its mass at the rim. Changing the mass distribution can also change a rigid system's spin, as angular momentum explains below.

Newton's second law for rotation

Top view: rope spins up a pottery wheel

Put the pieces together and you get the rotational twin of \(F_\text{net} = ma\). A net torque produces an angular acceleration, and rotational inertia sets how much:

\[\class{var-tau}{\tau_\text{net}} = \class{var-inertia}{I}\class{var-alpha}{\alpha}\]

Translational to rotational dictionary

\(F \to \tau\) force becomes torque
\(m \to I\) mass becomes rotational inertia
\(a \to \alpha\) acceleration becomes angular acceleration
\(\tau_\text{net} = 0\) \(\alpha = 0\): constant \(\omega\), rotational equilibrium balanced

Worked example: pottery wheel

Given solid disk, \(M = 4.0\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 0.5\ \text{m}\), rope pulls the rim tangentially with \(6.0\ \text{N}\)
Rotational inertia \(I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(4.0)(0.5)^2 = 0.50\ \text{kg·m}^2\)
Torque \(\tau = rF\sin 90^\circ = (0.5)(6.0) = 3.0\ \text{N·m}\)
Angular acceleration \(\alpha = \tau_\text{net}/I = 3.0/0.50 = 6.0\ \text{rad/s}^2\)

The solving strategy mirrors force problems exactly: draw the extended free-body diagram, sum torques about a smart axis, compute \(I\) for the shape, then solve \(\tau_\text{net} = I\alpha\). Once you have \(\alpha\), the rotational kinematic equations take over to give angles and angular speeds.

Angular momentum and angular impulse

The angular momentum of a rigid system about a chosen axis depends on its rotational inertia and angular velocity. Its direction lies along the axis and follows the right-hand rule:

\[\class{var-l}{L} = \class{var-inertia}{I}\class{var-omega}{\omega}\]

An angular impulse is the net torque multiplied by the time interval for which it acts. It points in the torque's direction and equals the rigid system's change in angular momentum:

\[\class{var-l}{\Delta\vec{L}} = \sum\class{var-tau}{\vec{\tau}}\,\Delta t\]

Variables

\(L\) angular momentum about the axis (kg·m²/s)
\(\Delta L\) change in angular momentum (kg·m²/s)
\(\Delta t\) time interval during which the net torque acts (s)

Direction and change

Same direction angular impulse, \(\sum\vec{\tau}\), and \(\Delta\vec{L}\) align
Torque with the spin increases the magnitude of \(L\) speeds up
Torque against the spin decreases the magnitude of \(L\) slows down

With no net external torque, angular momentum stays constant. A skater who pulls mass toward the axis lowers \(I\), so \(\omega\) rises to keep \(L = I\omega\) unchanged.

Key takeaways

Summary

Torque \(\class{var-tau}{\tau} = rF\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta}\), in N·m; counterclockwise positive
Lever arm \(r\sin\theta\), perpendicular distance from axis to the force's line of action
Extended free-body diagram draw the shape, attach forces where they act; gravity at the center of mass
Forces at the pivot \(r = 0\), so they exert no torque; choose the pivot as the axis to eliminate them
Rotational inertia \(\class{var-inertia}{I} = \sum mr^2\), in kg·m²; grows with mass far from the axis
Newton's second law, rotational \(\class{var-tau}{\tau_\text{net}} = \class{var-inertia}{I}\class{var-alpha}{\alpha}\), the rotational twin of \(F_\text{net} = ma\)
Rotational equilibrium \(\tau_\text{net} = 0 \implies \alpha = 0\); torques balance, \(\omega\) stays constant
Angular momentum \(\class{var-l}{L} = \class{var-inertia}{I}\class{var-omega}{\omega}\), about a specified axis; direction follows the right-hand rule
Angular impulse-momentum \(\class{var-l}{\Delta\vec{L}} = \sum\class{var-tau}{\vec{\tau}}\,\Delta t\); angular impulse points with the net torque
