Angular momentum of a point object

A point object carries linear momentum \(\class{var-p}{p} = m\class{var-v}{v}\), and it also carries angular momentum about any reference point you choose, even when nothing is spinning. It is built from that linear momentum and how far off-center the motion passes:

\[\class{var-l}{L} = r\,m\class{var-v}{v}\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta}\]

Variables

\(L\) angular momentum about the reference point (kg·m²/s)
\(r\) radial distance from the reference point to the object (m)
\(m\), \(v\) mass (kg) and speed (m/s) of the object
\(\theta\) angle between the radial direction \(\vec{r}\) and the velocity \(\vec{v}\)

Reading the angle

\(\theta = 90^\circ\) velocity perpendicular to \(\vec{r}\): \(L = rmv\) maximum
\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\) motion straight toward or away from the point: \(L = 0\) no angular momentum
Perpendicular distance \(r_\perp = r\sin\theta\), so \(L = r_\perp mv\)
Sign counterclockwise about the point is positive, clockwise negative

Only the piece of the velocity that curls around the reference point counts. A car driving straight at you head-on has plenty of momentum but zero angular momentum about you, because none of its motion sweeps around you.

Choosing the axis

Drone flying past a ground camera

Angular momentum is never a property of the object alone: it is a property of the object and the axis you measure it about. Move the reference point and \(r\) and \(\theta\) both change, so the same motion gets a different \(L\). The cleanest form uses the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of motion:

\[\class{var-l}{L} = r_\perp m\class{var-v}{v}, \qquad r_\perp = r\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta}\]

Same straight-line motion, different axes

Axis on the line of motion \(r_\perp = 0\), so \(L = 0\) zero
Axis off to the side \(L = r_\perp mv\), and it is constant as the object passes conserved
Axis twice as far off \(r_\perp\) doubles, so \(L\) doubles
Why it stays constant \(r\) grows while \(\sin\theta\) shrinks, and the product \(r\sin\theta\) never changes

So a question like "what is this object's angular momentum" is incomplete until an axis is named. Choose the axis deliberately: picking the point that the forces in your problem point toward is usually what makes \(L\) constant and the algebra short.

Angular momentum in orbit

Gravity on a satellite always points straight at the central object, so about that central object its lever arm is zero and it exerts no torque. With no net torque there is no angular impulse, so a satellite's angular momentum is constant in any orbit, circular or elliptical:

\[\class{var-l}{L} = r\,m\class{var-v}{v}\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta} = \text{constant} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad r_1\class{var-v}{v_1} = r_2\class{var-v}{v_2}\ \text{(at closest and farthest)}\]

Circular vs. elliptical

Circular orbit \(r\), \(v\), and \(\theta = 90^\circ\) are each separately constant \(L\) constant
Elliptical orbit \(r\), \(v\), and \(\theta\) all change, but the product does not \(L\) constant
Closest approach smallest \(r\), so largest \(v\); \(\vec{v}\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{r}\) here
Farthest point largest \(r\), so smallest \(v\); \(\vec{v}\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{r}\) here too

Worked example: comet swing

Given a comet passes closest to its star at \(r_1 = 8.0\times10^{10}\ \text{m}\) moving \(62\ \text{km/s}\), and reaches out to \(r_2 = 5.6\times10^{11}\ \text{m}\)
Both apsides use \(\theta = 90^\circ\) \(mr_1v_1 = mr_2v_2\); the mass cancels
Solve \(v_2 = v_1\dfrac{r_1}{r_2} = 62\left(\dfrac{8.0\times10^{10}}{5.6\times10^{11}}\right) = \dfrac{62}{7}\)
Result \(v_2 \approx 8.9\ \text{km/s}\) 7× farther, 7× slower

This is why comets whip through their closest approach in weeks and then crawl through the outer half of the orbit for decades. It is also Kepler's second law in disguise: constant \(L\) is exactly the statement that the line from star to comet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

Orbital energy

Satellite on an elliptical orbit

Gravity is a conservative force, so the satellite-central-object system's total mechanical energy is also constant. It is the satellite's kinetic energy plus the system's gravitational potential energy, measured with the zero of \(U_g\) infinitely far away:

\[\class{var-emech}{E} = \class{var-k}{K} + \class{var-u}{U_g} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\class{var-v}{v}^2 - G\frac{mM}{r}\]

For a circular orbit gravity supplies the centripetal force, \(mv^2/r = GmM/r^2\), which pins \(K\) to exactly half the magnitude of \(U_g\). That collapses the total into a single term:

\[\class{var-emech}{E} = -\frac{GmM}{2r} = -\class{var-k}{K} = \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-u}{U_g}\]

Circular vs. elliptical energy

Circular orbit \(K\) and \(U_g\) are each constant on their own both fixed
Elliptical orbit \(K\) and \(U_g\) trade back and forth every revolution sum fixed
Near the central object \(U_g\) is deeply negative and \(K\) is large
Far from it \(U_g\) climbs toward zero and \(K\) falls by exactly as much: \(\Delta K = -\Delta U_g\)
Bound orbit \(E < 0\) always; the satellite can never reach infinity trapped

Notice the strange bookkeeping of gravity: boosting a satellite to a higher circular orbit raises \(E\) toward zero but lowers its speed, because \(U_g\) climbs by twice as much as \(K\) falls. Mission planners burn fuel to slow a spacecraft down.

Escape velocity

Escape velocity is the speed at which the satellite-central-object system's mechanical energy is exactly zero. Zero is the break-even case: the object still has just enough kinetic energy to climb infinitely far, arriving there with nothing left over. Set \(K + U_g = 0\) and solve for \(v\):

\[\tfrac{1}{2}m\class{var-v}{v}^2 - G\frac{mM}{r} = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \class{var-v}{v_\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}\]

What the sign of \(E\) tells you

\(E < 0\) bound: a closed circular or elliptical orbit stays
\(E = 0\) exactly escaping: \(v \to 0\) as \(r \to \infty\) break-even
\(E > 0\) unbound: leaves with speed to spare and never returns gone
Independent of \(m\) the satellite's mass cancels, and so does launch direction

Worked example: breaking orbit

Given a \(620\ \text{kg}\) satellite circling Earth at \(r = 8.0\times10^{6}\ \text{m}\) (\(GM = 3.98\times10^{14}\))
Orbital speed \(v = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{4.98\times10^{7}} \approx 7.1\ \text{km/s}\)
Escape speed there \(v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt{2}\,v_\text{orbit} \approx 10.0\ \text{km/s}\)
Energy still needed lift \(E = -\dfrac{GmM}{2r} \approx -1.5\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\) up to \(0\)
So \(\Delta E = 1.5\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\), exactly the orbit's kinetic energy \(|E| = K\)

Escape velocity always beats orbital velocity at the same radius by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\), and it falls off as you get farther out, which is why a departing probe is cheaper to send from a high orbit than from the launch pad. The word "velocity" is a historical misnomer: only the speed matters, since aiming sideways instead of straight up changes the path but not the energy.

Key takeaways

Summary

Point-object angular momentum \(\class{var-l}{L} = rm\class{var-v}{v}\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta} = r_\perp mv\), in kg·m²/s; no spinning required
Axis dependence \(L\) is meaningless without a stated axis; on the line of motion \(L = 0\), off to the side \(L = r_\perp mv\)
Straight-line motion \(L\) about a fixed off-line point is constant, because \(r\sin\theta\) never changes
Orbital angular momentum gravity exerts no torque about the central object, so \(L\) is constant; at the apsides \(r_1v_1 = r_2v_2\)
Orbital energy \(\class{var-emech}{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\class{var-v}{v}^2 - G\dfrac{mM}{r}\) is constant in circular and elliptical orbits alike
Circular orbit shortcut \(\class{var-emech}{E} = -\dfrac{GmM}{2r} = -\class{var-k}{K} = \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-u}{U_g}\)
Escape velocity \(\class{var-v}{v_\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\,v_\text{orbit}\), the speed that makes \(E = 0\)
Two conserved quantities \(L\) fixes the orbit's shape and sweep rate; \(E\) fixes its size and whether it is bound at all
