Conditions for simple harmonic motion

An oscillation is any motion that repeats itself around an equilibrium position. It becomes simple harmonic motion only when the net force always points back toward equilibrium and grows in direct proportion to how far the object has been displaced:

\[F_\text{net} = -\class{var-spring}{k}\class{var-x}{x}\]

Variables

\(x\) displacement from equilibrium, not from the floor or the ceiling (m)
\(k\) stiffness of the restoring force, the proportionality constant (N/m)
minus sign the force opposes the displacement, so it is always a restoring force
\(A\) amplitude, the largest displacement reached on either side (m)

Is it simple harmonic?

Mass on an ideal spring \(F = -kx\) exactly yes
Pendulum at small angles restoring torque is proportional to \(\theta\) once \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) yes
Pendulum at large angles \(\sin\theta\) falls behind \(\theta\), so the period stretches only approximately
Ball bouncing on the floor restoring force is constant, not proportional to \(x\) no

That proportionality is what makes the motion "simple". Because \(F = ma\), it also means the acceleration is \(a = -(k/m)x\): largest at the turning points and exactly zero as the object flies through equilibrium, which is precisely where it moves fastest.

Spring-mass oscillators

Cart on a spring bumper

A block on a spring obeys Hooke's law by construction, so it is the cleanest oscillator there is. Solving \(ma = -kx\) gives a period set only by the mass and the stiffness:

\[\class{var-period}{T_s} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{\class{var-spring}{k}}}, \qquad \class{var-omega}{\omega} = \sqrt{\frac{\class{var-spring}{k}}{m}} = \frac{2\pi}{\class{var-period}{T}} = 2\pi f\]

What changes the period

Heavier block more inertia to turn around, so a longer period; \(4\times\) the mass gives \(2\times\) the period slower
Stiffer spring stronger restoring force, so a shorter period; \(9\times\) the stiffness gives \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) the period faster
Larger amplitude no change at all isochronous
Gravity no change; hanging the spring vertically only shifts where equilibrium sits no effect

Period, frequency, angular frequency

\(T\) seconds for one full cycle out and back (s)
\(f = 1/T\) cycles per second (Hz)
\(\omega = 2\pi f\) radians per second, the quantity that appears inside the sine and cosine (rad/s)
Reading a graph slope of \(T^2\) plotted against \(m\) is \(4\pi^2/k\), which is how \(k\) is measured from data

Amplitude independence is the surprising part. Pull the block twice as far and it has twice as far to travel, but the spring pulls twice as hard the whole way, and the two effects cancel exactly. This is why springs and pendulums make usable clocks while a bouncing ball does not.

Motion as a function of time

Because the acceleration is proportional to the negative of the displacement, the solution is a sinusoid. Starting the clock at maximum displacement gives the cosine form, and velocity and acceleration follow from its slopes:

\[\class{var-x}{x(t)} = \class{var-amp}{A}\cos(\class{var-omega}{\omega} t + \varphi), \qquad v(t) = -\class{var-amp}{A}\class{var-omega}{\omega}\sin(\class{var-omega}{\omega} t + \varphi), \qquad a(t) = -\class{var-amp}{A}\class{var-omega}{\omega}^2\cos(\class{var-omega}{\omega} t + \varphi)\]

Position, velocity, and acceleration of one oscillator

Reading the three curves

At \(x = \pm A\) \(v = 0\) and \(|a|\) is largest turning points
At \(x = 0\) \(|v| = A\omega\) is largest and \(a = 0\) equilibrium
Velocity vs. position shifted a quarter cycle ahead, since \(v\) is the slope of \(x\)
Acceleration vs. position an exact mirror image: \(a = -\omega^2 x\) at every instant
Phase constant \(\varphi\) slides the whole picture in time; it records where the object was when the clock started

Amplitude scales all three curves vertically without touching their spacing along the time axis. Only \(\omega\), fixed by the mass and stiffness, decides how tightly the cycles are packed.

Energy in an oscillator

With no friction, the spring-block system's total mechanical energy is fixed, and the two forms simply trade back and forth twice per cycle. Evaluating the total at a turning point, where \(v = 0\), is the fastest route to it:

\[\class{var-emech}{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\class{var-v}{v}^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-spring}{k}\class{var-x}{x}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-spring}{k}\class{var-amp}{A}^2\]

Where the energy sits

At \(x = \pm A\) all elastic potential: \(U_s = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\), \(K = 0\) all \(U\)
At \(x = 0\) all kinetic: \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\), \(U_s = 0\) all \(K\)
Maximum speed \(v_\text{max} = A\omega = A\sqrt{k/m}\)
Doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy, doubles the top speed, leaves the period alone \(E \propto A^2\)

Worked example: loaded cart

Given a \(0.80\ \text{kg}\) cart on a \(180\ \text{N/m}\) spring, pulled \(0.12\ \text{m}\) and released from rest
Total energy \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}(180)(0.12)^2 = 1.30\ \text{J}\)
Speed at equilibrium \(v_\text{max} = A\sqrt{k/m} = 0.12\sqrt{225} = 1.8\ \text{m/s}\)
Speed halfway out at \(x = A/2\), \(U_s\) is a quarter of \(E\), so \(v = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}v_\text{max} \approx 1.6\ \text{m/s}\) still 87%

Notice how flat that last result is. Halfway to the turning point the cart has given up only a quarter of its kinetic energy, so an oscillator spends most of its time near the ends of the swing, moving slowly, and blurs past the middle.

Simple pendulums

Lantern swinging from a rafter

A simple pendulum is a point mass on a massless string. Gravity supplies a restoring torque about the pivot, using the perpendicular component \(mg\sin\theta\) with lever arm \(L\):

\[\tau = -mgL\sin\class{var-theta}{\theta} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sin\theta\,\approx\,\theta\ }\; \tau \approx -mgL\class{var-theta}{\theta}\]

That approximation is the whole trick. Once \(\sin\theta\) is replaced by \(\theta\), the torque is proportional to the angular displacement, which is the rotational version of \(F = -kx\), and the motion is simple harmonic with:

\[\class{var-period}{T_p} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\]

The small-angle approximation

\(\theta = 5°\) \(\sin\theta\) and \(\theta\) agree to \(0.1\%\) excellent
\(\theta = 15°\) period runs about \(0.4\%\) long fine for most work
\(\theta = 45°\) period runs about \(4\%\) long no longer SHM
Why it works \(\theta\) must be in radians; \(\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + \dots\) and the correction is tiny while \(\theta\) is small

What changes the period

Longer string longer period; \(4\times\) the length gives \(2\times\) the period slower
Heavier bob no change; \(m\) sets both the torque and the rotational inertia and cancels no effect
Larger release angle no change while the angle stays small no effect
Weaker gravity longer period; the same pendulum on the Moon takes about \(2.5\times\) as long slower

Mass cancelling is the mirror image of free fall: a heavier bob is pulled harder but is equally harder to move. Because only \(L\) and \(g\) survive, a swinging pendulum is a direct measurement of local gravity, and plotting \(T^2\) against \(L\) gives a straight line of slope \(4\pi^2/g\).

Key takeaways

Summary

Condition for SHM \(F_\text{net} = -\class{var-spring}{k}\class{var-x}{x}\): restoring and proportional to displacement, so \(a = -\omega^2 x\)
Spring-mass period \(\class{var-period}{T_s} = 2\pi\sqrt{m/\class{var-spring}{k}}\), set by mass and stiffness only
Pendulum period \(\class{var-period}{T_p} = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\), set by length and gravity only
Amplitude never appears in either period; it scales speed and energy, not timing
Frequency relations \(\class{var-omega}{\omega} = 2\pi f = 2\pi/\class{var-period}{T}\)
Motion in time \(\class{var-x}{x} = \class{var-amp}{A}\cos(\class{var-omega}{\omega} t + \varphi)\), with \(v\) a quarter cycle ahead and \(a\) inverted
Oscillator energy \(\class{var-emech}{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-spring}{k}\class{var-amp}{A}^2\), all potential at the ends and all kinetic at the middle
Maximum speed \(v_\text{max} = \class{var-amp}{A}\class{var-omega}{\omega} = \class{var-amp}{A}\sqrt{\class{var-spring}{k}/m}\)
Small-angle approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) in radians is what turns a pendulum into a simple harmonic oscillator
Spring vs. pendulum mass matters for the spring and cancels for the pendulum; gravity is the reverse
