Fluids and density

A fluid is anything that flows and takes the shape of its container, so both liquids and gases count. Rather than track every particle, we describe a fluid by bulk properties, starting with density, the mass packed into each unit of volume:

\[\class{var-rho}{\rho} = \frac{m}{V}\]

Variables

\(\rho\) density (kg/m³)
\(m\), \(V\) mass (kg) and volume (m³) of the sample
Fresh water \(1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\), the reference every other value is judged against
Air, seawater, mercury about \(1.2\), \(1025\), and \(13600\ \text{kg/m}^3\)

The ideal fluid model

Incompressible density is the same everywhere and never changes assumed
Non-viscous no internal friction, so no mechanical energy is lost to drag assumed
Real fluids gases compress easily and honey is thick with viscosity model breaks down
Why it is fair water barely compresses at all, so liquids fit the model closely

Density is what decides whether something floats, not weight. A shipping container of foam outweighs a marble and still floats, because floating is a competition between two densities rather than two weights.

Pressure

Fluid particles are constantly colliding with any surface they touch. Averaged over a surface, those countless tiny collisions add up to a steady perpendicular force, and pressure is that force spread over the area it acts on:

\[\class{var-press}{P} = \frac{F_\perp}{\class{var-area}{A}}\]

Variables and units

\(P\) pressure, in pascals (\(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2\))
\(F_\perp\) force component perpendicular to the surface (N)
\(A\) area the force is spread over (m²)
\(1\ \text{atm}\) \(1.013\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}\), about \(101\ \text{kPa}\)

Key properties

Pressure is a scalar it has no direction of its own scalar
The force it makes is not always perpendicular to the surface and pointing into it
Acts in every direction a point in a fluid is squeezed equally from all sides isotropic
Same depth, same pressure two connected points at one depth are always at one pressure

Dividing by area is why a thumbtack works. Your thumb and the tack's point carry the same force, but the point concentrates it into a tiny area, so the pressure there is enormous.

Pressure with depth

Sensor lowered into a harbor

Anything at depth \(h\) has to hold up the entire column of fluid stacked above it, and that column's weight is \(\rho g h\) per unit of area. So pressure climbs steadily as you descend, starting from whatever is pressing down on the surface:

\[\class{var-press}{P} = \class{var-press}{P_0} + \class{var-rho}{\rho} g h\]

Absolute vs. gauge

Absolute pressure the full \(P = P_0 + \rho g h\), measured from a true vacuum
Gauge pressure \(P_\text{gauge} = P - P_0 = \rho g h\), the amount above atmospheric
A tire read as flat gauge pressure zero, absolute pressure still \(1\ \text{atm}\) not a vacuum
Which to use gauge for anything that has atmosphere on both sides, absolute inside Bernoulli

What depth pressure ignores

Shape of the container a narrow pipe and a wide lake match at equal depth no effect
Total volume of fluid no effect at all no effect
Depth below the surface linear increase, the only geometric thing that matters matters
Sideways position no effect; move horizontally and the pressure is unchanged no effect

Only the vertical drop counts, which is why a dam's thickness is set by the water's depth rather than by how far the reservoir stretches behind it. It is also why divers descending just \(10\ \text{m}\) of water already double the pressure on their bodies.

The buoyant force

Since pressure grows with depth, the fluid pushes harder on the bottom of a submerged object than on its top. Those two pushes do not cancel, and the leftover is the buoyant force, always pointing straight up:

\[\class{var-fb}{F_b} = \class{var-rho}{\rho_\text{fluid}}\, V_\text{disp}\, g\]

Variables

\(F_b\) buoyant force, always upward (N)
\(\rho_\text{fluid}\) density of the fluid, not of the object (kg/m³)
\(V_\text{disp}\) volume of fluid pushed out of the way, so only the submerged part (m³)
In words Archimedes' principle: the upward push equals the weight of the displaced fluid

Reading the formula

Object's own density never appears; it decides the weight, not the buoyant force absent
Fully submerged \(V_\text{disp}\) equals the object's whole volume, so \(F_b\) stops growing with depth
Deeper is not stronger both faces gain the same extra pressure, so the difference is unchanged depth-independent
Denser fluid larger \(F_b\) for the same object, which is why saltwater floats you higher stronger

A hollow steel hull floats for exactly this reason. Shaping the steel around a large volume of air makes the displaced water heavier than the ship without adding any mass to it.

Floating and sinking

Cargo crate released in a harbor

A floating object settles at the depth where buoyancy exactly balances weight. Setting \(\rho_\text{fluid}V_\text{disp}g = \rho_\text{obj}V_\text{obj}g\) and cancelling \(g\) leaves a result with no masses in it at all:

\[\frac{V_\text{disp}}{V_\text{obj}} = \frac{\class{var-rho}{\rho_\text{obj}}}{\class{var-rho}{\rho_\text{fluid}}}\]

Three cases

\(\rho_\text{obj} < \rho_\text{fluid}\) floats, with that density ratio as the submerged fraction floats
\(\rho_\text{obj} = \rho_\text{fluid}\) hovers anywhere it is placed neutral
\(\rho_\text{obj} > \rho_\text{fluid}\) sinks; the floor supplies the missing upward force sinks
Apparent weight a submerged object reads \(W - F_b\) on a scale, never its true weight

Worked example: floating buoy

Given a \(0.50\ \text{m}^3\) buoy of density \(300\ \text{kg/m}^3\) in seawater (\(1025\ \text{kg/m}^3\))
Weight \(m = \rho_\text{obj}V = 150\ \text{kg}\), so \(W = 1470\ \text{N}\)
Submerged fraction \(\dfrac{300}{1025} = 0.293\) 29% under
Check \(F_b = 1025(0.293 \times 0.50)(9.8) = 1470\ \text{N}\), matching the weight exactly

An iceberg makes the point vividly: ice at \(917\ \text{kg/m}^3\) in seawater sits with \(89\%\) of itself below the surface. The visible tip is the small remainder, and the ratio is fixed by the two densities alone.

Key takeaways

Summary

Density \(\class{var-rho}{\rho} = m/V\) in kg/m³; water is \(1000\), the benchmark for everything else
Ideal fluid incompressible and non-viscous, so density is uniform and no energy is lost to drag
Pressure \(\class{var-press}{P} = F_\perp/\class{var-area}{A}\) in pascals; a scalar that pushes perpendicular to every surface
Pressure with depth \(\class{var-press}{P} = \class{var-press}{P_0} + \class{var-rho}{\rho}gh\); depth is all that matters, not shape or volume
Gauge pressure \(P_\text{gauge} = \rho g h\), the reading above atmospheric rather than above vacuum
Buoyant force \(\class{var-fb}{F_b} = \class{var-rho}{\rho_\text{fluid}}V_\text{disp}\,g\), the weight of the displaced fluid, always upward
Floating condition \(V_\text{disp}/V_\text{obj} = \rho_\text{obj}/\rho_\text{fluid}\); denser than the fluid means it sinks
Apparent weight \(W - F_b\) for anything submerged, which is why lifting a rock underwater feels easy
What buoyancy ignores the object's density and its depth; only fluid density and displaced volume enter
