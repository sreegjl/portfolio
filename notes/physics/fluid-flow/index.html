fluid flow & bernoulli's principle

what drives flow, volume flow rate, the continuity equation, bernoulli's equation, and its standard special cases
study mode

What makes a fluid flow

A parcel of fluid is pushed from behind and pushed back from in front. It only accelerates if those two pushes differ, so a pressure difference is what drives flow, exactly as a net force drives any other acceleration:

\[F_\text{net} = (\class{var-press}{P_1} - \class{var-press}{P_2})\class{var-area}{A}\]

Ideal flow assumptions

Steady the speed at any fixed point never changes with time assumed
Laminar smooth layers that never mix, so no turbulence assumed
Incompressible density is uniform and constant assumed
Non-viscous no internal friction, so mechanical energy is conserved assumed

Reading a pressure difference

Higher pressure behind net force points forward, so the fluid speeds up accelerates
Higher pressure ahead net force points backward, so the fluid slows down decelerates
Equal pressures constant speed along that stretch no change
Gravity also pushes flow along, which is why water runs downhill without a pump

Everything that follows is these assumptions plus two conservation laws: mass is conserved, giving the continuity equation, and energy is conserved, giving Bernoulli's equation.

Flow rate and continuity

Water through a narrowing pipe

Volume flow rate is how much fluid passes a cross-section each second. Nothing piles up and nothing vanishes in an incompressible fluid, so whatever enters one end of a pipe must leave the other at the same rate:

\[Q = \class{var-area}{A}\class{var-v}{v} = \text{constant} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \class{var-area}{A_1}\class{var-v}{v_1} = \class{var-area}{A_2}\class{var-v}{v_2}\]

Variables

\(Q\) volume flow rate (m³/s)
\(A\) cross-sectional area of the pipe (m²), \(\pi r^2\) for a round pipe
\(v\) flow speed averaged across that cross-section (m/s)
Where it comes from conservation of mass, once density is the same at both ends

Consequences

Narrow section smaller \(A\) forces larger \(v\) faster
Halving the radius quarters the area, so the speed goes up fourfold \(v \propto 1/r^2\)
Wide section the fluid slows and the streamlines spread apart slower
Streamline spacing crowded streamlines mean fast flow, which is how flow pictures are read

Pinching a garden hose is continuity in your hand. The tap still delivers the same litres per second, so squeezing the exit area is the only way to make the water leave faster.

Bernoulli's equation

An ideal fluid loses nothing to friction, so the work done by pressure and gravity goes entirely into kinetic energy. Writing that energy balance per unit of volume gives a quantity that is the same everywhere along a streamline:

\[\class{var-press}{P_1} + \class{var-rho}{\rho} g y_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-rho}{\rho}\class{var-v}{v_1}^2 = \class{var-press}{P_2} + \class{var-rho}{\rho} g y_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-rho}{\rho}\class{var-v}{v_2}^2\]

What each term is

\(P\) the pressure itself, which is energy stored per unit volume (J/m³ = Pa)
\(\rho g y\) gravitational potential energy per unit volume, with \(y\) measured from any level you pick
\(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2\) kinetic energy per unit volume, the dynamic pressure
The sum constant along a streamline; it is conservation of energy with volume divided out

Using it safely

Same streamline the two points must be connected by the flow required
Open to the air the pressure there is atmospheric, not zero
Same height the \(\rho g y\) terms cancel, leaving faster flow at lower pressure the principle
Zero speed the equation collapses back to \(P = P_0 + \rho g h\) for a still fluid

The famous half of this is the trade between speed and pressure at one height: where a fluid runs fast, its pressure drops. That is what lifts a wing, pulls a shower curtain inward, and lets a spray bottle draw liquid up its tube.

Bernoulli in practice

Water jet from a punctured tank

A tank open at the top with a hole in its side is the standard application. Both the surface and the hole sit at atmospheric pressure, and a wide tank's surface barely drops, so the pressure and \(v_1\) terms fall away and only the height difference survives:

\[\class{var-v}{v} = \sqrt{2gh}\]

Reading the result

Density is absent it divides out, so oil and water leave at the same speed no effect
Same as free fall identical to a drop released from height \(h\), which is why it is called Torricelli's result
Deeper hole faster jet, but it also has less height left to fall faster
Farthest reach a hole halfway up the water column throws the jet the furthest

Worked example: narrowing pipe

Given level water pipe narrowing from \(A_1 = 0.040\ \text{m}^2\) to \(A_2 = 0.010\ \text{m}^2\), with \(v_1 = 1.5\ \text{m/s}\)
Continuity \(v_2 = v_1\dfrac{A_1}{A_2} = 1.5(4) = 6.0\ \text{m/s}\)
Bernoulli, level pipe \(P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = 500(36 - 2.25)\)
Result \(P_1 - P_2 = 16.9\ \text{kPa}\) narrow section is lower

Notice the asymmetry in that example: the speed rose by a factor of four, but the pressure drop depends on the squares, so it grew sixteenfold. Squeezing a pipe too hard can pull the pressure low enough to boil the liquid, which is how pumps cavitate and damage themselves.

Key takeaways

Summary

What drives flow a pressure difference across a parcel, \(F_\text{net} = (\class{var-press}{P_1} - \class{var-press}{P_2})\class{var-area}{A}\)
Ideal flow steady, laminar, incompressible, non-viscous; the last two are what make the equations work
Volume flow rate \(Q = \class{var-area}{A}\class{var-v}{v}\) in m³/s
Continuity \(\class{var-area}{A_1}\class{var-v}{v_1} = \class{var-area}{A_2}\class{var-v}{v_2}\) from conservation of mass; narrow means fast, and \(v \propto 1/r^2\)
Bernoulli's equation \(\class{var-press}{P} + \class{var-rho}{\rho}gy + \tfrac{1}{2}\class{var-rho}{\rho}\class{var-v}{v}^2\) is constant along a streamline, from conservation of energy
Bernoulli's principle at one height, faster flow sits at lower pressure
Still fluid set every \(v = 0\) and Bernoulli reduces to \(\class{var-press}{P} = \class{var-press}{P_0} + \class{var-rho}{\rho}gh\)
Tank with a hole \(\class{var-v}{v} = \sqrt{2gh}\), independent of density and equal to free-fall speed from \(h\)
Two laws, two equations continuity fixes the speeds from the geometry; Bernoulli then fixes the pressures
