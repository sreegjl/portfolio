Types of collisions

Collisions are classified by what happens to kinetic energy, which can be converted to heat, sound, and deformation. Total momentum is conserved in every type:

Types of collisions

Elastic \(K\) conserved objects bounce apart, no energy lost
Inelastic \(K\) decreases objects bounce apart, some energy dissipated
Perfectly inelastic max \(K\) lost objects stick together and share one final velocity
Explosion / separation reverse of perfectly inelastic, \(K\) increases from stored energy, \(\vec{p}\) still conserved

In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects move off together, so conservation of momentum alone fixes the final velocity:

\[\vec{v}_f = \dfrac{m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i}}{m_1 + m_2}\]

In a 1D elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved, which pins down both final velocities:

\[v_{1f} = \dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} \qquad v_{2f} = \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}\]

Elastic collision limits (target at rest)

\(m_1 = m_2\) objects swap velocities: \(v_{1f} = 0\), \(v_{2f} = v_{1i}\)
\(m_1 \gg m_2\) heavy object barely slows, light one flies off at \(\approx 2v_{1i}\)
\(m_1 \ll m_2\) light object bounces back at \(\approx -v_{1i}\), heavy one barely moves

Try the sliders below: set the masses, initial velocities, and elasticity, then watch the collision. Total momentum is identical before and after in every case, but kinetic energy survives only when the collision is elastic.

Two carts colliding on a track

A quick elastic test: relative speed of approach equals relative speed of separation, \(v_{1i} - v_{2i} = -(v_{1f} - v_{2f})\). Separating slower than approaching means the collision was inelastic.

Particle accelerators

Collisions between high-speed particles reveal the fundamental building blocks of the universe. To produce them, physicists build particle accelerators, the largest of which is the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN, a \(27\,\text{km}\) loop buried under the border of France and Switzerland.

The Large Hadron Collider

Beams two proton beams travel in opposite directions in parallel pipes
Speed accelerated to nearly the speed of light over many laps
Guidance magnetic fields steer the protons, electric fields accelerate them
Collisions the beams cross at set locations, giving protons a chance to collide
Detectors layered instruments like the CMS record the directions and energies of what emerges

The collisions themselves are far too small to see. Physicists reconstruct them from detector data using the same two tools that govern macroscopic collisions: conservation of momentum and conservation of energy.

Missing momentum

Conservation of momentum applies to particle collisions exactly as it does to colliding carts: with no unbalanced external force, the system's total momentum is constant, in each dimension separately:

\[\class{var-p}{\vec{p}}_{\,\text{total,i}} = \class{var-p}{\vec{p}}_{\,\text{total,f}}\]

Before an LHC collision, both protons travel straight along the beam line, so the total momentum in the plane perpendicular to the beam is zero. After the collision it must still be zero.

Reading a collision event

Detected particles charged-particle tracks and energy deposits in the detector layers
Balance check detected momenta should sum to zero in the transverse plane
Missing momentum imbalance points to one or more particles that escaped undetected
Likely culprit neutrinos no charge, very little mass, barely interact with matter

Try the sliders below: aim the two detected particles and see what it takes to balance the event. Whenever the detected momenta do not sum to zero, something invisible carried away the difference.

Balancing a detector event

Neutrinos pass through the detectors without leaving a trace, but they cannot hide completely: the momentum they carry away shows up as the missing piece of the balance.

Relativistic momentum

LHC particles move at nearly the speed of light, so special relativity is needed to describe them. Many relativistic quantities involve the Lorentz factor:

\[\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}\]

The relativistic momentum of a particle is:

\[\class{var-p}{\vec{p}} = \gamma m\vec{v}\]

Variables

\(\gamma\) Lorentz factor, dimensionless, \(\geq 1\)
\(v\) particle speed (m/s)
\(c\) speed of light, \(3.00 \times 10^8\) m/s
\(m\) particle mass (kg)

Limits

\(v \ll c\) \(\gamma \approx 1\) reduces to the familiar \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\)
\(v \to c\) \(\gamma\) grows without bound full equation required
Either case momentum is still a conserved, vector quantity

The familiar \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) is just the slow-speed limit of the relativistic definition: when \(v \ll c\), the ratio \(v^2/c^2 \approx 0\) and \(\gamma \approx 1\).

Relativistic energy

Total energy is also conserved in particle collisions. The relativistic energy of a particle depends on both its momentum and its mass:

\[E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\]

Variables

\(E\) total relativistic energy (J)
\(p\) magnitude of the particle's momentum (kg·m/s)
\(m\) particle mass (kg)
\(c\) speed of light (m/s)

Two contributions

\(p^2c^2\) energy from the particle's motion
\(m^2c^4\) energy from the particle's mass
At rest \((p = 0)\) \(E = mc^2\), pure mass energy
Massless \((m = 0)\) \(E = pc\), so \(p = E/c\)

Setting \(p = 0\) gives the famous mass energy equation, the energy an object has at rest purely due to its mass:

\[E = mc^2\]

Setting \(m = 0\) instead describes massless particles like photons:

\[E = pc\]

Photons have no mass, yet they still carry both energy and momentum, so they participate fully in both conservation laws. The relativistic equations hold for slow macroscopic objects too, but there \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) and \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\) are simpler and accurate enough.

Creating new particles

In a macroscopic inelastic collision, lost kinetic energy becomes disorganized internal energy of the objects' particles, and the total mass is the same before and after. In particle physics the accounting is more dramatic: mass is not conserved. Only the total relativistic energy \(E\) is.

Macroscopic vs. particle collisions

Momentum conserved in both, vector sum never changes
Total energy conserved in both, only its form changes
Mass constant for macroscopic objects, not conserved in particle collisions
"Lost" kinetic energy becomes heat, sound, and deformation, or the mass energy of new particles

This is why the protons must move so fast: only when the system carries enough energy of motion can some of it convert into the rest mass of new, heavier particles. That conversion is how the LHC produces particles that do not exist in everyday matter.

Key takeaways

Summary

All collisions conserve momentum, only elastic ones conserve kinetic energy
Perfectly inelastic objects stick, \(\vec{v}_f = (m_1\vec{v}_{1i} + m_2\vec{v}_{2i})/(m_1 + m_2)\)
Elastic test relative speed of approach equals relative speed of separation
Conservation of momentum \(\class{var-p}{\vec{p}}_{\,\text{total,i}} = \class{var-p}{\vec{p}}_{\,\text{total,f}}\), holds in particle collisions, each component separately
Missing momentum an unbalanced event reveals undetected particles, like neutrinos
Lorentz factor \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\), \(\approx 1\) when \(v \ll c\), grows as \(v \to c\)
Relativistic momentum \(\class{var-p}{\vec{p}} = \gamma m\vec{v}\), reduces to \(m\vec{v}\) at low speed
Relativistic energy \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\), motion term plus mass term
Mass energy \(E = mc^2\) for a particle at rest
Massless particles \(E = pc\), photons carry energy and momentum without mass
Mass vs. energy mass is not conserved in particle physics, total energy always is
